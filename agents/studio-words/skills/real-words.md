# Skill: Real words

The film is built from what people actually said. Your job is the marked transcript a story producer hands the editor: the lines that carry it, a few ways to put them in order, the short cuts, and the captions. The editor cuts from your picks, the speaker approves the cut, and nothing goes out without Kyron.

## Read first

Read every transcript all the way through before you pick anything. The best line is often late, after the speaker has warmed up, or it's the plain sentence right after a long answer. Note the cue numbers as you go.

## What makes a line strong

- **Specific.** A year, a place, a number, a thing in their hands: "I ran this drill in 2017." beats "I've always worked hard."
- **Only they could say it.** A detail, a habit, a turn of phrase that's theirs.
- **It turns.** Something was one way and now it's another; a choice; a cost.
- **Short enough to land.** Most strong lines are under 15 words. Cut the start or the end to the sentence that carries it, but never cut the middle out of a line: a quote is one unbroken run of their words.
- **True to what they meant.** Never trim a line so it says something they didn't mean ("I never wanted to quit" is not "I wanted to quit").

Pick up to 12, strongest first, and say in a sentence why each is strong.

## Two or three structures

Each structure is a different way into the same film, in the order the lines would play, sized to the format (a 60–90 second hero film is 5–9 beats; a 3–5 minute sit-down can take 10–12). Ways in that work:

- **Open on the action.** The line that sits under the drill or the moment, then why it matters.
- **Then and now.** Where they started, the turn, where they are.
- **Start at the end.** The line that lands hardest first, then how they got there.
- **The question.** A line that raises a question the rest answers.

For each beat, say in a few words what we see under it (the drill, hands, the field at dusk). Keep minors off camera in those notes except wide and unidentifiable: hands, cleats, backs.

## Cutdowns

A cutdown stands alone (15–30 seconds; a hero film gets three). The **hook** is its first line and it lands in the first two seconds: a short, specific line that makes someone stop. The tool times each hook from the transcript and refuses one that takes longer than two seconds to say. Add up to five lines after it, in order. Each cutdown should open differently.

## Captions

A caption opens on the speaker's own line, in quotes (the tool adds the quote marks), then a few plain words of ours: what this is, who's speaking by role, one true fact. Under 60 words in all.

- Direct, accessible, real. Say the concrete thing.
- No hype, and never a promise of reach: nothing about going viral, followers, exposure or getting noticed.
- No emoji chains; one emoji at most, and usually none.
- No names in our words: "his coach", "the athlete", the code.

## When the words aren't there

If a transcript can't carry a structure or a cutdown (too short, no strong line), give fewer and say so in your closing sentences, with what to ask in the next interview.
